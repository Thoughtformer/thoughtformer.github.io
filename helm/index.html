How can AI-assisted work remain accountable to people?

Helm is how I am exploring that question through building, testing, and documenting a small automation system. I am sharing the work as a learning journey, including the parts that have not worked.

Why this project

Practical experience.
A learner's mindset.

I am Aaron Spoonts. My background spans technical systems, electronics repair and refurbishment, data, and creative technology. I am working toward a meaningful career transition into AI-related work.

I tend to stay with difficult problems, listen to feedback, and keep searching for a workable solution. Helm gives that persistence a direction while I develop my programming skills, research methods, and understanding of AI governance and safety.

My role: setting direction and constraints, testing workflows, reviewing decisions, identifying failures, and guiding revisions. AI tools contribute substantially to implementation and writing, including this presentation. I do not claim independent authorship of every implementation or established AI research expertise.

Research questions

What I want to understand.

Who actually has authority?

Can a model propose work without being able to expand its own tool, network, or file permissions?

What does approval authorize?

Can a person's approval stay bound to the exact consequential action they reviewed?

What counts as success?

How do we distinguish a confident statement, a completed request, a checked result, and something useful?

Does the extra machinery help?

Does orchestration improve outcomes when resource access, human effort, cost, latency, and failures are counted fairly?

Can a person understand the limits?

Does the interface make permissions, incomplete outputs, and uncertainty clear enough for meaningful oversight?

Can we learn from inconvenient results?

Can failed attempts remain visible and change the next decision, instead of being rewritten as progress?

Selected observations

What has challenged the assumptions.

These are summaries of private development records. This public showcase does not include the implementation or raw evaluation data, so the observations cannot be independently reproduced from this page. They are not new results from publishing this presentation.

A functioning runtime did not establish a capable worker.

The project developed bounded execution and evidence paths while general worker competence remained unqualified. The lesson: runtime success and task correctness need separate evidence.

A planning response did not produce a usable plan.

A request ended at its output limit. Later local validation work and synthetic tests did not establish successful live planning. The lesson: incomplete output must remain visibly incomplete.

An apparent advantage had confounded conditions.

Early bounded workflow comparisons included unequal tool access and narrow fixtures. The lesson: those observations cannot support general superiority or economic-savings claims.

Passing tests did not establish an accepted product.

Interface and provider work coexisted with installer restrictions and wider test failures. The lesson: a usable experience needs its own evidence.

Limits in view.

Helm is the project and human-facing layer. Escapement is its underlying control layer. The implementation reuses upstream software, including StarNet, with its own attribution boundaries.

This is an exploratory project, not a finished safety system, peer-reviewed publication, compliance certification, or production-ready general agent platform. Readiness remains unknown and adaptive routing is not qualified.

Here, governance means practical authority and accountability in a small system. It does not establish institutional policy expertise or a solution to frontier AI risks.

I want to grow through careful experiments, better programming, and feedback from experienced people. The aim is to become useful through the work—not to present the learning as already complete.
