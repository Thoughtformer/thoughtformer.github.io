AI systems research: what worked, what did not.

I investigated how AI-assisted automation could remain accountable to people through explicit permissions, human approval, and checked results. The work produced narrow prototypes and unresolved limitations. After competitive and feasibility review, I retained it as research rather than continue presenting it as a standalone product.

Why this project

Practical experience.
A learner's mindset.

My background spans technical systems, electronics, design, and creative technology. I began this work by asking whether existing computers and open-source tools could support useful AI-assisted work without disproportionate cost or complexity.

The project moved from broad automation ambitions toward narrowly testable questions about authority, independent verification, actual usability, and economic value. I eventually concluded that a specific market need had not been validated.

My role: setting direction and constraints, testing workflows, reviewing decisions, identifying failures, and guiding revisions. AI tools assist with implementation and writing, including this presentation. I do not claim independent authorship of every implementation or established AI research expertise.

Research questions

What the work investigated.

Who actually has authority?

Can a model propose work without being able to expand its own tool, network, or file permissions?

What does approval authorize?

Can a person's approval stay bound to the exact consequential action they reviewed?

What counts as success?

How do we distinguish a confident statement, a completed request, a checked result, and something useful?

Does the extra machinery help?

Does orchestration improve outcomes when resource access, human effort, cost, latency, and failures are counted fairly?

Can a person understand the limits?

Does the interface make permissions, incomplete outputs, and uncertainty clear enough for meaningful oversight?

Can we learn from inconvenient results?

Can failed attempts remain visible and change the next decision, instead of being rewritten as progress?

Selected observations

What has challenged the assumptions.

These are summaries of private development records. This public showcase does not include the implementation or raw evaluation data, so the observations cannot be independently reproduced from this page. They are not new results from publishing this presentation.

A functioning runtime did not establish a capable worker.

The project developed bounded execution and evidence paths while general worker competence remained unqualified. The lesson: runtime success and task correctness need separate evidence.

A planning response did not produce a usable plan.

A request ended at its output limit. Later local validation work and synthetic tests did not establish successful live planning. The lesson: incomplete output must remain visibly incomplete.

An apparent advantage had confounded conditions.

Early bounded workflow comparisons included unequal tool access and narrow fixtures. The lesson: those observations cannot support general superiority or economic-savings claims.

Passing tests did not establish an accepted product.

Interface and provider work coexisted with installer restrictions and wider test failures. The lesson: a usable experience needs its own evidence.

Selected milestones

A record of work and revision.

  1. September 2026

    Started with existing resources.

    Explored practical automation and upstream software reuse before adding more infrastructure.

  2. Early October 2026

    Built bounded control and workflow prototypes.

    Developed execution, verification, and interface work while preserving unknown qualification states and documenting failures.

  3. October 2026

    Encountered the gap between prototypes and usefulness.

    Recorded incomplete planning, confounded comparisons, and installation limits rather than treating them as solved.

  4. October 18, 2026

    Made the learning purpose explicit.

    Published this curated account of the questions, contribution, AI assistance, and limitations. This is a documentation milestone, not a new experiment.

  5. November 2026

    Reconsidered the product direction.

    Competitive overlap, limited resources, unresolved usability, and lack of a validated niche led me to retain the engineering as research rather than market an unproven product. No new experiment was performed for this decision.

Scope and honesty

Research retained.
Limits in view.

The project was historically labeled Helm, with an internal control layer labeled Escapement. These are historical code and documentation references, not prospective public brands. The private implementation reused upstream software, including StarNet, with its own attribution and license boundaries.

This is an exploratory project, not a finished safety system, peer-reviewed publication, compliance certification, or production-ready general agent platform. Readiness remains unknown and adaptive routing is not qualified.

Here, governance means practical authority and accountability in a small system. It does not establish institutional policy expertise or a solution to frontier AI risks.

There is no validated standalone business offering or accepted product roadmap. Reuse of the engineering would require a real customer problem and new evidence that it adds value. I directed the questions, constraints, testing, and revisions with substantial AI assistance in engineering and documentation.
